# What to give me for a deck

Plain-language guide for the person asking for the deck. None of it has to be tidy — bullet
points in a message are fine. The more of the first section you answer, the less guessing
happens.

## The four that matter

1. **Who is in the room, and what do you want them to do afterwards?** A finance-ministry
   briefing and an internal work-in-progress deck get different decks from the same analysis.
2. **Your key messages, in your words.** One sentence each, three to seven of them. These
   become the slide titles, so write them as *findings* ("Exports pull away after 2018"),
   not topics ("Exports").
3. **How long.** Minutes of talking time, or a slide count. Ten minutes is roughly 8-12
   slides.
4. **Where the evidence lives.** Paths to scripts, notebooks, exported figures, or data. I
   will look for figures the analysis already produces before making new ones.

## Useful if you have it

- **A storyline**, if you already know the order. If you do not, I will propose one and you
  edit it — that is usually faster than starting from a blank page.
- **Figures you already like**, and any you want left out.
- **Numbers that must appear exactly** (headline figures, table values) and their source
  line as you want it printed.
- **Language** — one per deck; both if you need an English and a Spanish version of the
  same slides.
- **A standalone deck or a section?** By default I build a *section* (no cover, no closer),
  because sections get compiled into a larger presentation later. Say if you want a
  standalone deck with a title slide.
- **Anything already decided** — a slide that must stay as-is, an order that is fixed, a
  chart your co-author insists on.

## What you get back

An editable `.pptx` on the GL template, plus a slide-by-slide summary and any open
questions. Nothing is final: open it in PowerPoint and change whatever you like — that is
the point of building a pptx rather than a PDF.

## What I will push back on

- **A slide saying two things.** It becomes two slides, or one of them goes.
- **A title that names a topic instead of stating a finding.** Slide titles carry the
  argument; if the title is "Exports", the audience has to work out why they are looking at
  it.
- **A table nobody can read from the back of the room.** Roughly 6 rows by 4 columns is the
  limit; past that it gets charted or split.
- **A chart with more than a few colors.** Six is the hard ceiling, and most charts want
  two: the focus series, and everything else in grey.
- **Text shrunk to fit.** If body text has to go below the recipe's size to fit on the
  slide, the slide has too much on it.

That pushback is a conversation, not a veto — if you have a reason, say so and we build it
your way.
